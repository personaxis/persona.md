# Brand Strategy

> Reference for the CMO persona. Compiled from Marty Neumeier's *The Brand Gap* (2003) and *Zag* (2006), Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) and *How Brands Grow Part 2* (2015), Wally Olins' *On Brand* (2003), and Les Binet & Peter Field's *The Long and the Short of It* (2013).

Brand is what people believe about you when you are not in the room. It is built by the cumulative effect of every artifact, interaction, and silence the company produces. The CMO treats brand as a long-cycle asset that compounds when invested in and erodes when neglected, never as a logo or a tagline.

---

## 1. What brand actually is

Marty Neumeier's working definition:

> *"A brand is a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or organization. Brand is not what you say it is. It is what they say it is."*

Three implications:

1. **Brand lives in the receiver, not the sender.** Marketing influences but does not control it.
2. **Brand is the sum of all signal.** Product behavior, support response, pricing, copy, design, hiring, founder communication — all of it builds or erodes brand.
3. **Brand is asymmetric.** Years of investment can be erased by a single high-profile failure. The CMO budgets for protection, not only growth.

## 2. Byron Sharp's mental and physical availability

Sharp's empirical work at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute reframes brand growth in terms of two factors:

### 2.1 Mental availability

The probability that a buyer thinks of the brand in a buying situation. Increased by:

- **Reach** — exposing the brand to as many category buyers as possible
- **Distinctive brand assets** — colors, sounds, characters, layouts, vocabulary that are unique and instantly attributable
- **Category entry points (CEPs)** — the triggers, situations, and needs that prompt buying

A buyer who thinks of three brands in a category will buy from one of them. A buyer who thinks of two will buy from one of two. Marketing's job is to be one of the brands the buyer thinks of.

### 2.2 Physical availability

The ease of buying. In SaaS, this maps to:

- **Findable** — search presence, comparison-site presence, marketplace presence
- **Try-able** — free trial, freemium, sandbox, demo
- **Buy-able** — frictionless purchase, multiple billing options, regional pricing
- **Use-able** — onboarding that lands the activation event

Sharp's argument is that mental and physical availability matter more than positioning, loyalty, or persuasion. The CMO invests in both, even when the data team is biased toward persuasion-style A/B tests.

### 2.3 Distinctive brand assets

Distinctive ≠ differentiated. Distinctive means *instantly attributable to your brand*. The CMO maintains an asset inventory:

| Asset | Distinctiveness score | Attribution score |
|---|---|---|
| Color palette | 0.8 | 0.7 |
| Logo mark | 0.9 | 0.95 |
| Typography | 0.4 | 0.3 |
| Sonic mark | n/a | n/a |
| Spokesperson | 0.6 | 0.5 |
| Category vocabulary | 0.7 | 0.6 |
| Layout pattern | 0.5 | 0.4 |

Assets below 0.6 attribution are candidates for retirement or reinvestment.

## 3. The 60/40 brand-vs-activation split

Les Binet and Peter Field's IPA research on B2B and B2C campaigns concluded that the optimal split of marketing budget is **60% brand building (long-term)** and **40% sales activation (short-term)**.

| | Brand building | Sales activation |
|---|---|---|
| **Time horizon** | 6–24 months | 0–3 months |
| **Audience** | Category buyers (mostly not in-market) | In-market buyers |
| **Mechanism** | Mental availability, distinctive assets, narrative | Lead capture, conversion, pricing offers |
| **Measurement** | Brand tracking, share of search, organic growth | CAC, conversion rate, LTV/CAC |
| **Failure mode if over-weighted** | Pipeline drought | Diminishing returns, brand erosion |

The CMO budgets defend both halves explicitly. The CFO will always pressure toward the activation half because its returns are measurable on a quarterly window. The CMO documents the brand-building thesis and the lead measures (aided recall, share of search, organic-to-paid ratio) so the long-cycle investment can be defended.

## 4. Brand strategy artifacts

The CMO maintains a small set of brand artifacts. Each is locked, dated, and versioned.

### 4.1 The brand platform

```
Purpose: Why we exist beyond making money
Mission: What we are doing right now
Vision: The future state we are building toward
Values: How we behave
Promise: What the customer can count on
Story: The narrative arc of how we got here and where we are going
```

### 4.2 The brand voice guide

Tone descriptors with examples and counter-examples. The CMO writes voice guides in the form of "We are X, not Y" because contrast is the only way the team learns to apply it.

```
We are direct, not blunt.
We are warm, not casual.
We are confident, not arrogant.
We are technical, not jargon-heavy.
We are practical, not cynical.
```

Voice guides ship with:
- 20 *do* / *don't* sentence pairs
- Banned phrases (category clichés, dark-pattern verbs, sycophancy)
- Required vocabulary (category terms the company is investing in owning)

### 4.3 The visual system reference

Owned by design but reviewed by marketing. The CMO ensures the visual system supports the distinctive-asset goals from section 2.3.

### 4.4 The brand metrics dashboard

- **Aided brand recall** (in-category survey)
- **Unaided brand recall** (in-category survey)
- **Share of search** (your brand searches / category searches, via Google Trends)
- **Organic-to-paid traffic ratio** (proxy for owned demand)
- **Direct traffic share** (proxy for owned attention)
- **Net Promoter Score / customer satisfaction** (proxy for promise delivery)
- **Earned media volume and sentiment** (proxy for cultural capital)

The CMO reports the brand dashboard alongside the demand dashboard. The two are measured at different cadences and on different time horizons.

## 5. Wally Olins on brand architecture

For companies with multiple products or sub-brands, the CMO chooses an architecture:

- **Monolithic** (Google, FedEx): one brand across all products. Maximizes mental availability. Risks: a failure in one product hits the master brand.
- **Endorsed** (Polo by Ralph Lauren): sub-brand exists, parent brand endorses. Balances clarity with reuse.
- **Branded house** (Procter & Gamble): each product has its own brand identity, parent is invisible. Maximizes flexibility. Risks: marketing cost multiplies.

The CMO defaults to monolithic for startup-stage companies because mental-availability accumulation is the constraint. Architecture changes are deferred to scale-stage.

## 6. The category narrative as brand asset

A category narrative is the highest-leverage brand asset a B2B company can build. It is:

- A *worldview* — the company's argument about how the work in the category should be done
- A *vocabulary* — named problem, named solution, named alternative behavior
- A *cast* — buyer protagonist, status-quo antagonist, future state, first-mover hero

The CMO ships the category narrative through:

- A founder-authored long-form essay or manifesto
- A keynote-format presentation given by the CEO repeatedly
- Recurring narrative beats in content (posts, podcasts, talks)
- Sales-enablement materials that anchor every pitch in the narrative
- A POV document customers and partners can cite

A category narrative that holds for 3+ years compounds into category leadership.

## 7. Defending brand against short-term pressure

The recurring CMO challenge: a CFO asking why the brand-building half of the budget is not producing measurable pipeline this quarter. The CMO's defense:

1. **Pre-commit lead measures.** Aided recall, share of search, organic-to-paid ratio. Numbers that move in 30–90 days even when pipeline does not.
2. **Pre-commit kill criteria.** If aided recall is flat in 6 months, the campaign is reviewed.
3. **Document the asymmetry.** A brand-building win compounds for years. A brand-building loss is a $0 line item. The expected value is positive even with low hit rates.
4. **Show the counterfactual.** Pure activation budgets produce escalating CAC over 12 months. Show the CAC trajectory with and without brand investment.

The CMO who cannot defend the brand-building half loses it. The company then loses the asset over the following two years and does not connect the cause to the effect.

## 8. Common brand failure modes

- **Treating brand as the logo.** The visual system is a small fraction of brand. Voice, narrative, behavior, and product experience are larger.
- **Confusing distinctive with differentiated.** Distinctive assets do not differentiate; they make the brand findable. Differentiation is positioning's job.
- **Letting performance marketing erode brand.** Aggressive performance copy that contradicts the brand voice produces short-term conversion lift and long-term brand erosion.
- **Refreshing brand for the sake of refreshing.** Distinctive-asset accumulation requires continuity. A rebrand resets the clock.
- **Underspending on reach.** B2B marketers chronically over-invest in conversion and under-invest in category-buyer reach. The 95-5 rule (95% of category buyers are not in-market at any given moment) argues for reach.
