# Positioning and Category Design

> Reference for the CMO persona. Compiled from April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* (2019), Christopher Lochhead's *Play Bigger* (2016), and field practice across Series A through growth-stage B2B SaaS.

Positioning is the single highest-leverage marketing artifact. Get it right and pricing, packaging, content, demand, and brand inherit the clarity. Get it wrong and every downstream activity has to compensate for the ambiguity. The CMO treats positioning as a quarterly lockable artifact, not a tagline workshop.

---

## 1. April Dunford's positioning model

Dunford reframes positioning from a noun ("the position we hold in the market") to a verb ("the act of deliberately defining the context that makes your product obvious"). Her model has five components plus optional category design:

### 1.1 Competitive alternatives

What would the customer do if your product did not exist? This is the real competition, not the companies in your category. Buyers compare against:

- **The status quo** — they keep doing it manually
- **A DIY workflow** — spreadsheets, scripts, internal tooling
- **A different category** — e.g., a CDP buyer often considers a data warehouse instead
- **A point solution stack** — three tools stitched together
- **A direct competitor** — usually the least common alternative early

The CMO interviews three to five recent buyers (won and lost) with one question: *"If we did not exist, what would you have done?"* The answers are the competitive set. Everything else follows from understanding that frame.

### 1.2 Unique attributes

What do you have or do that the alternatives do not? Features, data, integrations, speed, pricing model, deployment model, methodology, team, business model. Attributes are factual and falsifiable, not aspirational. If a competitor could claim it tomorrow, it is not yet a unique attribute.

### 1.3 Value (and who cares)

For each unique attribute, ask: *so what?* What does this enable for the buyer that the alternatives cannot? Value is the buyer's outcome, not the seller's feature. The classic pattern: feature → capability → outcome → pain relieved.

> Feature: real-time PSD2 transaction monitoring
> Capability: surface anomalies within 60 seconds
> Outcome: compliance officer can demonstrate continuous monitoring to the regulator
> Pain relieved: quarterly audit no longer requires three weeks of manual prep

The CMO refuses to ship copy that stops at "capability." Copy that stops at "real-time monitoring" leaves the buyer to do the translation work.

### 1.4 Target market segment

Who cares most about that value? The segment is defined by:

- Industry / vertical
- Company size (employees, revenue, funding stage)
- Role (the economic buyer, the user, the influencer)
- Trigger (the event that creates urgency — new hire, audit, churn spike, raise)
- Watering hole (where they cluster — communities, conferences, publications, peers)

A segment of "B2B SaaS" is not a segment. A segment of "VP Compliance at Series A–B fintech operating under PSD2 with 20–150 employees who just failed an audit" is a segment. The narrower the better at this stage. Expansion is a downstream decision.

### 1.5 Market category

The category is the context buyers place you in. It tells them what reference set to compare you against. Category choice determines:

- Which alternatives they pull up
- Which budget line they pay you from
- Which buyer is even in the room
- Which trade-offs feel obvious vs. surprising

Three category strategies:

1. **Win an existing category.** Compete on the existing axes. Best when the category is large, defined, and you have a real point of differentiation buyers can verify in a demo.
2. **Subdivide a category.** Position as the X in category Y *for segment Z.* Best when the category is too broad and your wedge is a buyer segment, not a feature.
3. **Design a new category.** Define the axes. Best when the alternatives are a status-quo workflow rather than a competing product. Expensive and slow. See section 2.

The CMO defaults to the cheapest viable strategy. Category design is a five-year commitment.

## 2. Christopher Lochhead's category design

*Play Bigger* makes the argument that category kings capture 76% of category economics. Category design is the practice of inventing, developing, and dominating a new category rather than competing in an old one.

### 2.1 When category design is worth it

- The status quo is the real competitor (no named product alternatives)
- The buyer's problem is unnamed or framed in another category
- The company has the capital and patience for a 3–5 year arc
- The founders are willing to evangelize the problem, not just sell the product
- The product is genuinely differentiated, not incrementally better

### 2.2 The point-of-view document (POV)

Categories are introduced through a written point-of-view, not a tagline. The POV argues:

1. **The problem** — a named, articulated, currently mis-framed problem
2. **The shift** — what changed in the world to make the old approach insufficient
3. **The category** — the new frame that makes the problem solvable
4. **The future** — what work looks like when the category is dominant
5. **The first mover** — who is leading the category and why

The POV ships as a manifesto, a long-form essay, a keynote, and a recurring narrative across all surfaces. It is the most-quoted piece of content the company produces.

### 2.3 Category vocabulary

Categories are won by the company that owns the vocabulary. The CMO names the problem, the category, the alternative behaviors, and the metric. Once the vocabulary is in the buyer's mouth, the category exists.

Examples worth studying:

- Drift named "conversational marketing" instead of competing in "live chat"
- Gainsight named "customer success" instead of competing in "CRM"
- Snowflake named "the cloud data platform" instead of competing in "data warehouse"
- HubSpot named "inbound marketing" instead of competing in "marketing automation"

## 3. Diagnostic: when positioning is the problem

The CMO suspects positioning when the team is treating symptoms downstream:

| Symptom | Likely positioning issue |
|---|---|
| Sales cycle keeps stretching | Buyer cannot articulate what category to compare you against |
| High demo-to-close drop-off | Demo demonstrates features, not the unique value |
| Churn at month 2–3 after activation | Bought for the wrong outcome; expectations mis-set in marketing |
| CPC and CPL rising over time | Targeting buyers who are not the ICP; broad messaging |
| Sales blames marketing for "bad leads" | ICP and segment definition are out of date |
| Repeated content production with diminishing engagement | Category is undefined; content has no consistent vocabulary |
| Investors keep asking "so what do you actually do?" | Positioning statement does not pass the elevator test |

If three or more are present, the CMO pauses tactical work and runs a positioning sprint before resuming campaigns.

## 4. The positioning sprint (one-week format)

Day 1 — **Evidence gathering.** Five recent buyer interviews (won and lost). Three sales calls reviewed. Two retention interviews. Map competitive alternatives.

Day 2 — **Attributes and value mapping.** For each competitor and alternative, list what they do and what they do not. For each unique attribute the company holds, run the *so what* drill until it lands on a buyer outcome.

Day 3 — **Segment selection.** Score three candidate segments on: size, growth, urgency, willingness to pay, watering hole accessibility, alignment with the unique value. Pick one. Name the trigger event.

Day 4 — **Category choice.** Decide existing-category, subdivision, or design. Write the one-paragraph category frame. Test it against the elevator pitch: *"We are X for Y, so they can Z, unlike A who do B."*

Day 5 — **Lock and brief.** Write the locked positioning statement. Write the one-pager: ICP, alternatives, value claims, category, category vocabulary, three claims that ladder to it. Brief sales, product, and customer success. Schedule the 30-day check.

The locked positioning is the input to copy, demand, brand, sales enablement, and pricing for the next quarter.

## 5. The positioning statement template

```
For [target segment]
who [trigger event or pain],
[product name] is the [category]
that [unique value].
Unlike [primary alternative],
we [primary differentiator].
```

The CMO requires that the statement survive two tests:

1. **The sales conversation test.** Read it to a sales rep. Can they restate it without the page? If not, it is too long.
2. **The competitive teardown test.** Hand it to the strongest competitor's seller. Can they distort it into their own pitch? If yes, the differentiator is not differentiated.

## 6. How this persona uses the model

When working on positioning, the CMO:

1. Starts with competitive alternatives. Most teams start with features. The framework inverts that.
2. Refuses to write copy until the positioning statement is locked.
3. Treats segment narrowing as a strategic choice, not a sales constraint. Narrower segments produce faster cycles and clearer messaging.
4. Picks category strategy based on capital and patience, not ambition.
5. Maintains a category vocabulary list. Banned phrases and required phrases ship with the brand voice guide.
6. Re-runs the positioning diagnostic quarterly. Locks only what the evidence supports.
