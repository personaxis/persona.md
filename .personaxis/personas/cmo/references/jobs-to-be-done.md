# Jobs-to-be-Done

> Reference for the CMO persona. Compiled from Clayton Christensen's *Competing Against Luck* (2016), Tony Ulwick's *Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice* (2016), Bob Moesta's switch-interview methodology, and Alan Klement's *When Coffee and Kale Compete*.

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is a theory of customer behavior. It argues that customers do not buy products; they hire products to make progress in their lives. The CMO uses JTBD as the upstream artifact for positioning, messaging, product feedback, and pricing. When marketing speaks in features, the team is selling tools. When marketing speaks in jobs, the team is selling progress.

---

## 1. The core thesis

> *"People do not want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole."* — Theodore Levitt
>
> *"Customers do not want a quarter-inch hole. They want to hang a picture of their kid."* — Clayton Christensen extending Levitt

A job is the progress a customer is trying to make in a specific circumstance. It includes:

- The **functional dimension** — the practical outcome
- The **emotional dimension** — how the customer wants to feel
- The **social dimension** — how the customer wants to be perceived

A milkshake at 8am for a commuter is a different job than a milkshake at 4pm for a parent with a child. Same product, two jobs. The categories — breakfast, snack, dessert — are about the product. The jobs are about the customer.

## 2. Two complementary lineages

JTBD splits into two schools that the CMO uses for different purposes:

### 2.1 The Christensen / Moesta school — switch interviews

This school treats jobs as **causal stories**. Methodology: interview customers who recently switched (bought your product, switched from your product, or chose your product over an alternative) and reconstruct the timeline of the decision.

The interview maps **four forces**:

1. **Push of the situation** — what made the old solution insufficient
2. **Pull of the new solution** — what attracted the customer to the new approach
3. **Anxiety of the new** — what made the switch scary
4. **Allegiance to the old** — what kept them from switching sooner

The buying decision happens when Push + Pull > Anxiety + Allegiance.

Switch interviews produce a story, not a list. The CMO uses them to:

- Discover the real trigger event (often very specific: a missed audit, a new hire, a churn spike)
- Identify the actual alternative the customer rejected (often not the named competitor)
- Find the language the customer used (used directly in copy)
- Locate the anxiety the customer felt (addressed directly in onboarding and sales conversation)

Switch interviews ship as audio recordings, transcripts, and a four-forces canvas per customer.

### 2.2 The Ulwick / Strategyn school — outcome-driven innovation

This school treats jobs as **stable, measurable, and decomposable**. Methodology: identify the job-to-be-done, then enumerate the customer's **desired outcomes** (statements like *"minimize the time it takes to..."* or *"minimize the likelihood that..."*).

Each outcome is scored on:

- **Importance** — how much the customer cares
- **Satisfaction** — how well current solutions deliver

Outcomes with high importance and low satisfaction are **opportunities**. Outcomes with high importance and high satisfaction are **table stakes**. The CMO uses outcome maps to:

- Build the value proposition (lead with under-served outcomes)
- Sequence the roadmap (product investment goes to the opportunities)
- Position against alternatives (where competitors are over-serving or under-serving)
- Inform pricing (charge for the outcomes that move the needle on importance)

Outcome maps ship as quantitative artifacts with importance/satisfaction scatter plots.

## 4. How JTBD shapes positioning, messaging, and demand

**Positioning.** The competitive set in Dunford's framework is the *alternative solutions* in the JTBD canvas. The unique value is the *under-served outcomes* the product addresses. The segment is the ICP for whom the job is most important and least satisfied.

**Messaging.** Copy uses the customer's language from the switch interviews. Headlines describe the job and the desired outcome, not the product. Subheads describe the capability that delivers the outcome.

**Demand.** Channel selection is anchored in the job's *trigger event*. Where is the customer when the trigger fires? What are they searching for? Who do they ask? The CMO refuses to buy a channel without a thesis about how the channel intercepts the trigger.

**Pricing.** Willingness to pay is tied to the importance score on the desired outcome. Pricing tiers map to job sizes (small job → individual; mid job → team; large job → enterprise).

**Sales enablement.** Sales reps are trained on the four forces, not on the product. Discovery questions surface the push, pull, anxiety, and allegiance.

**Onboarding and lifecycle.** The first 30 days target the anxiety force surfaced in the switch interviews. The first 90 days target the next-job-to-be-done that creates expansion.

## 5. Common JTBD failure modes

- **Confusing jobs with personas.** A persona is a *who*. A job is a *what they are trying to do*. The same persona has multiple jobs across the day.
- **Confusing jobs with use cases.** A use case is product-centric ("the user uses our reporting feature to..."). A job is customer-centric ("when I am preparing for the board, I want to...").
- **Over-decomposing the job.** Customers do not hire products for one micro-task. They hire them for a job large enough to create switching cost. If the job decomposes to a single feature, expand the frame.
- **Skipping the four forces.** Push and pull are obvious. Anxiety and allegiance are where the deal is actually decided. Skipping them ships marketing that addresses only the positive case.
- **Treating jobs as static.** Jobs evolve as the customer's context evolves. A startup compliance officer's job at 20 employees is different at 200. Re-interview annually.

## 6. Working JTBD example

**Job:** "When my fintech is approaching its first regulatory audit, I want to demonstrate continuous compliance monitoring, so I can clear the audit without diverting two engineers for three weeks and without flagging us as a high-risk reviewee for next year."

- **Push:** failed last audit; new regulator scrutiny; cofounder anxiety
- **Pull:** specific monitoring tool that integrates with existing transaction infra; saw a competitor avoid the same trap
- **Anxiety:** "what if the tool flags false positives and I cannot defend them?"; "what if my engineers are uncomfortable with the integration?"
- **Allegiance:** "we already paid the consultant for the year"; "the spreadsheet works, just slowly"

Marketing implication: copy leads with audit clearance, not with monitoring features. Onboarding addresses false-positive handling within the first week. Sales is trained to surface the existing consultant relationship and reframe (not displace) it.
