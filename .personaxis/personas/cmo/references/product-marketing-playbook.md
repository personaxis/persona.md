# Product Marketing Playbook

> Reference for the CMO persona. Compiled from the Product Marketing Alliance's *Product Marketing Foundations* curriculum, April Dunford's *Sales Pitch* (2023), Yi Lin Pei's writing on launches at scale, and field practice across B2B SaaS launches from seed to growth stage.

Product marketing is the discipline that connects what is built to what is sold. The CMO owns it directly until the company can support a dedicated head of PMM (typically post-Series-A). PMM ships the launch narrative, the sales pitch, the competitive teardown, the win/loss analysis, and the product-led messaging that anchors the whole funnel.

---

## 1. The five PMM responsibilities

The Product Marketing Alliance frame:

1. **Insight** — own the buyer, the market, and the competition as primary sources of truth
2. **Pricing and packaging** — input to product and finance on tier design and price points
3. **Positioning and messaging** — the locked artifact that the rest of marketing inherits
4. **Launches** — the sequenced shipping of new product into the market
5. **Sales enablement** — the materials, training, and reinforcement that let sales execute the messaging

The CMO ensures each responsibility has an owner (a person, a contractor, or themselves), a cadence, and an artifact.

---

## 2. The launch tier framework

Not every release is a launch. Distinguishing **release** from **launch** is the highest-leverage PMM decision the CMO makes.

| Tier | Trigger | Scope | Investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — Marquee** | New category, new ICP, or material brand-defining moment | Press, analyst, executive keynote, customer cohort, integrated campaign | 6-12 weeks of PMM time, 4-6 weeks of CMO time |
| **Tier 2 — Major** | Significant feature, expansion-relevant capability, or competitive response | Dedicated landing page, blog, email campaign, sales enablement, in-product surfacing | 3-6 weeks of PMM time |
| **Tier 3 — Standard** | New feature with measurable customer impact | Changelog post, in-product surfacing, customer email | 1-2 weeks of PMM time |
| **Tier 4 — Continuous** | Iterative improvements, bug fixes, minor capability | Changelog entry only | <1 day of PMM time |

The CMO defends the tiering against the recurring temptation to over-launch. Over-launching dilutes attention; under-launching wastes engineering investment. Quarterly, a team typically ships ~1 Tier 1, ~2-3 Tier 2, ~6-10 Tier 3, and many Tier 4.

## 3. The launch brief

The PMM-owned, CMO-approved artifact that every launch starts from. Required sections:

```
1. Launch tier (1-4) and rationale
2. The buyer and the job-to-be-done this addresses
3. The status quo and the alternatives this displaces
4. The customer story (the 3-sentence narrative)
5. The unique value claim (and the evidence behind it)
6. The category frame (where this lives in the buyer's mental model)
7. Pricing and packaging implications
8. Lead measure (movement expected in first 30 days)
9. Lag measure (movement expected by 90 days)
10. The launch sequence: what ships when, to whom
11. Risks and mitigations
12. Decision rights and escalation
13. Owners and dates
```

The brief is 1-3 pages, written in prose, reviewed by product, sales, and CS before any campaign asset is built.

## 4. Launch sequencing — the 6-week template

For a Tier 1 or Tier 2 launch:

| Week | Internal milestone | External milestone |
|---|---|---|
| **-6** | Launch brief signed; PMM lead assigned | — |
| **-5** | Customer narrative drafted; 5 customer interviews scheduled | — |
| **-4** | Messaging matrix locked; sales pitch drafted | Beta customers briefed (under NDA if needed) |
| **-3** | Landing page in design; email sequence in copywriting | Analyst pre-briefings (Tier 1 only) |
| **-2** | Sales enablement complete; in-product surfacing built | Press pre-briefings (Tier 1 only) |
| **-1** | Internal launch readiness review; sales certification | Customer launch-day comms drafted |
| **0** | Launch day | Press, blog, email, in-product, social all live |
| **+1** | First lead-measure reading | Demand-engine adjustments based on lead measure |
| **+4** | Lag-measure reading | Customer testimonials captured for next launch |

The CMO enforces the calendar. A launch slipping a week is acceptable. A launch slipping a quarter is a sign the brief was wrong.

## 5. The sales pitch (April Dunford's *Sales Pitch* model)

Dunford's argument: most sales decks are pitch-template-driven (problem, solution, demo, pricing). The right deck is positioning-driven and earns the right to discuss the product. Structure:

1. **The insight** — the worldview the company holds that the buyer may not. ("Compliance reporting is treated as a quarterly cost when it should be treated as a continuous capability.")
2. **The alternatives** — what buyers do today and why each falls short
3. **The perfect world** — what the buyer's life looks like when the problem is solved
4. **Introducing the product** — what category it fits in, what unique value it delivers
5. **Proof** — customer evidence, demo of the differentiated capability
6. **The ask** — the specific next step

Every customer-facing deck in the company inherits this structure. Variant decks for AE-led, SDR-discovery, and executive-briefing differ in length, not in structure.

## 6. Competitive enablement

The CMO maintains a competitive battlecards repository, not as a sales weapon but as a positioning artifact. Per competitor:

- **Their positioning statement** (as they say it, as the market hears it)
- **Their ICP** (overlap with ours, divergence from ours)
- **Their unique attributes** (real ones, not marketing claims)
- **Their weaknesses** (what they cannot do, what they do poorly)
- **The buyer's perception** (research-backed, not internal opinion)
- **The objection patterns** (what buyers ask about them in our cycles)
- **The trap question** (a discovery question that surfaces a structural weakness)
- **The reframe** (how we shift the buyer from their frame to ours)

Battlecards are updated quarterly. The CMO bans "we are better than X at everything" framing because it erodes credibility. Real positioning concedes ground intentionally.

## 7. Win-loss analysis

The PMM-owned learning loop. Every closed deal (won or lost) over $25K ACV gets a 30-minute conversation with the buyer:

- What problem were you trying to solve?
- What alternatives did you consider?
- What was the moment you decided to proceed (or not)?
- What did our team do well? What did they do poorly?
- What would have changed your decision?

The CMO codes results into a quarterly win-loss report covering:

- Why deals are won (top 3 reasons)
- Why deals are lost (top 3 reasons, broken into competitor-loss, no-decision, and budget-loss)
- Where the positioning is landing
- Where the pricing is friction
- Where the sales motion is failing
- What the product roadmap should hear

The report is read aloud at the CMO + CRO + CPO monthly review. Action items have owners.

## 8. Sales enablement

Enablement is not a slide deck delivered once. It is an ongoing system:

### 8.1 Enablement artifacts

| Artifact | Purpose | Update cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Sales pitch (deck) | Positioning-anchored buyer narrative | Quarterly |
| Discovery framework | Surface the JTBD four forces | Quarterly |
| Demo flow | Show the differentiated capability in 7 minutes | Per major launch |
| Battlecards | Competitive positioning | Quarterly |
| Objection handler | Top 15 objections, scripted responses | Quarterly |
| ROI calculator | Quantify the buyer outcome | Per pricing change |
| Customer story library | 6-12 stories tagged by ICP, segment, use case | Continuous |
| Pricing reference | Tier structure, discount ceiling, packaging rules | Per pricing change |

### 8.2 Enablement cadence

- **Onboarding** — every new AE/SE goes through the full enablement curriculum in week 1-2
- **Launch certification** — every AE certified on a new launch within 1 week of launch day
- **Quarterly refresh** — 90-minute session per quarter on what changed and why
- **Field reality check** — quarterly listening to 5 sales calls per AE to find the gap between enablement and execution

The CMO co-owns enablement with the CRO. Marketing builds the materials; sales delivers them.

## 9. Customer marketing

Once a customer is in seat, the PMM motion continues. The CMO runs four customer-marketing programs:

1. **Reference program** — recruit, train, and route customer references for net-new sales
2. **Case studies** — 6-12 per year, one per major ICP segment, professionally produced
3. **Customer advisory board** — quarterly meeting of top-tier customers shaping roadmap and narrative
4. **Customer-led content** — customer-authored blog posts, podcast appearances, conference talks

Customer marketing is the highest-trust demand creation channel. Buyers believe customers more than they believe marketing.

---

## 10. Common PMM failure modes

- **Launching everything as Tier 1.** Dilutes attention; degrades the launch infrastructure.
- **Brief written after the asset is built.** Inverts the dependency; asset shapes the brief instead of the brief shaping the asset.
- **Sales enablement as a single onboarding session.** Materials decay in 90 days without reinforcement.
- **Win-loss skipped because it is uncomfortable.** Loses the highest-quality learning signal in the business.
- **Battlecards written as marketing aspiration, not buyer reality.** Sales stops using them within a quarter.
- **Competitive positioning that concedes nothing.** Buyers see through it; sales loses trust.
- **PMM treated as a content team.** PMM is positioning, packaging, launches, and enablement. Content is downstream.
